## 01 — Moderator: opening

Welcome to the group report on our research catalog: U.S. incidents involving minors and gender-related actions taken without a parent's knowledge, without consent, or over a parent's objection. A note before we start: these are AI-generated voices reading the findings of seven research tracks, checked through November 18, 2026.

Here is the headline. We looked for any documented case where a school, or a school employee, arranged gender-related medication or surgery for a minor, without the relevant parent knowing or consenting, and without a prior court order. Across all seven tracks, we found no case that meets all four of those elements. That describes what our searches turned up. It is not proof that no such case exists.

What we did find is a large body of school social-transition disputes, a smaller set of court and child-welfare cases, and several near misses. Let's start with the central question.

## 02 — Researcher: first-priority medical question

Thanks. I ran the nationwide search on that central question, and I tested each candidate against the four elements separately. The closest cases each fail somewhere specific.

In Maine, in a case called Lavigne, a school social worker allegedly gave a 13-year-old chest binders and said he wouldn't tell the parents. But a binder is a device, not medication or surgery, and the facts were never adjudicated. The case was dismissed on other grounds, and the Supreme Court declined review in March 2026.

In Arcadia, California, a teenager received testosterone while in foster care. But by the mother's own account, a judge authorized it, so the no-court-order element fails.

In Massachusetts, a child-welfare agency reportedly sought testosterone, a juvenile court temporarily blocked it, and no treatment is established.

In Colorado and Washington, counselors allegedly made referrals or handed out clinic lists. A referral is not treatment.

I also checked the fact-checks. CNN and PolitiFact found no case. When asked for examples, the Trump campaign, and later the White House, pointed only to social-transition cases. And I found no criminal charge, license action, or firing of school staff for supplying hormones.

## 04 — Researcher: Orange County, Pasadena, Santa Clarita

I covered Orange County, plus Pasadena and Santa Clarita. Nothing in my area involves medication or surgery. I found three individual incidents, all about names, pronouns, or information withheld from parents.

First, a Pasadena family, called the Does, who are plaintiffs in the statewide Mirabelli case. Their sworn account is that a school used a male name for their daughter, and the principal wouldn't confirm it.

Second, a Santa Clarita mother known as Lydia. Her account is partly corroborated by a 2023 memo from the district's own law firm, which records the student using a new name at school. The rest remains her allegation, and no lawsuit was filed.

Third, an Orange County parent in the Huntington Beach lawsuit, in an unnamed district. That one is a complaint allegation only.

For Newport-Mesa and Irvine specifically, I found no lawsuit and no identifiable family. In Newport-Mesa, a parent group claimed that 23 students had gender support plans without informed parental consent. The district denied it, and I could not find the outcome of the outside investigation it announced. So those two districts sit in the policy file, not the incident file.

## 05 — Researcher: California school litigation

I took the California litigation. Same bottom line: these are social-transition and non-disclosure cases, with no blockers, hormones, or surgery alleged.

One correction to our earlier notes. The Poe family in Mirabelli is not an Escondido family. They're tied to Clovis Unified, and then to Yosemite Valley Charter. That's one family at successive schools, not two incidents. Their sworn account is that staff used a male name from August 2022 without telling them, and that they learned of it from a hospital doctor in September 2023. That account has been recited by the courts, but it has not been tested against the schools.

On status: in December 2025, the district court issued a statewide injunction. On March 2, 2026, the Supreme Court let it take effect for parents only. The teachers' portion stays on hold, and the Ninth Circuit has not ruled on the merits.

In Chico, the Regino case was dismissed again in January 2026 and is on a second appeal. Everything about that child is still a complaint allegation.

And in Spreckels, the Konen case settled for about $100,000, with an express denial of liability. A settlement like that does not establish what happened.

## 06 — Researcher: school cases outside California

Outside California, I reviewed 17 cases involving an individual child. None alleges that a school arranged medication or surgery.

What's notable is how few have actual findings. Two rulings rest on a factual record, and they point in opposite directions. In Kettle Moraine, Wisconsin, a court ruled for the parents in 2023. There, the parents knew, objected explicitly, and the district followed the student anyway. In Wyoming, a court ruled for the district in 2025, finding no evidence that anyone lied or concealed anything. I read that ruling through the district's brief and press coverage, not the order itself.

Some parent accounts were contradicted by records. In a Florida case, the mother's own emails show she knew about the name and pronoun request, although a planning meeting held without the parents is not disputed.

By evidence label, in this collection: 3 established by records, 3 corroborated accounts, 1 sworn but contested, and 10 reported allegations. That's a count of our collection, not a national total.

At the Supreme Court, four petitions from these cases were denied, and none has been granted for argument.

## 07 — Researcher: providers, child welfare, courts

I covered medical providers, child welfare, and family courts. This is where medical treatment actually appears. And in every case I verified, a court, an agency, or a state law stood behind it.

In San Diego, a case called In re D.H. A juvenile court authorized testosterone for a 17-year-old in foster care, before treatment began. The mother was notified and objected. The father agreed.

In Contra Costa County, the Hudacko case. The custody order gave the mother sole legal custody, and allowed hormone therapy if the university clinic recommended it. The father says an implant counted as surgery, which the order barred. No court has decided that, and a Supreme Court petition is pending.

On the Arcadia case, the mother's 2020 complaint does not mention a school at all.

The nearest miss I found is from Minnesota, a case called Calgaro. Providers treated a minor on her own consent under state law, with no parental consent and no court order. That's three of the four elements. But the school's documented role was refusing the mother access to records, not arranging treatment.

## 08 — Researcher: legislation and minor-consent laws

On legislation, I kept laws separate from incidents, because a statute allowing minor consent is not evidence that any treatment happened.

Maine is the only state I found whose law names this directly. Since October 2023, a 16 or 17-year-old can consent to hormones or puberty suppression under set conditions, including a diagnosis, and the minor's report that a parent refused to support treatment. Surgery is excluded.

Oregon has a general rule from 1971, letting minors 15 and older consent to medical treatment. It is not specific to gender care.

California has no law letting an ordinary minor consent to hormones or blockers.

One correction. The Vermont act in our notes is real, but it's a 2026 child-welfare agency law, not a gender-care law.

In Congress, three House-passed bills are waiting in Senate committees, including a criminal ban and a school parental-consent bill. And one tracker counts 28 states with restrictions on this care for minors.

My search allowance ran out before I could check pending bills in several states, including New York, Illinois, and Maryland. Those are gaps, not findings.

The board met on November 17, but I could not verify the vote. The minutes should settle it, likely in December.

On the organizational map, the documented ties are legal representation, bill authorship, and friend-of-the-court briefs, on both sides. I found no documentation of funding or coordination between groups.
