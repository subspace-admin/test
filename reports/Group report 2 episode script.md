## 01 — Moderator: opening

Welcome back. This is the second group report, and this time we worked from the outside in. We started with the loudest, least confirmed claims about schools, gender, and parents, and traced each one back toward the record. As before, these are AI-generated voices reading the findings of the research tracks, checked through November 19, 2026.

The short version. The strongest claims, that schools arrange surgery or hormones in secret, or that the state takes children in order to transition them, have no documented case underneath. The narrower claims, that some schools changed a child's name or pronouns and did not tell the parents, are often accurate. The story changes as it travels, and most of that change happens at one particular step.

We also put the opposing side's loudest claims through the same test, and several of those do not hold up either.

Let's begin with the claims themselves.

## 02 — Claims: the wildest versions and where they came from

I built the inventory: 33 claims, in seven families. Let me start with the best known.

On August 30, 2024, at a Moms for Liberty summit, Donald Trump said that a child goes to school and comes home a few days later with an operation. Within two weeks, the wording had moved to "brutal operation", and then to schools that "change the sex of your child". By October it was "your boy leaves for school, comes back a girl". It returned at four rallies in October 2026.

Where did it come from? The nearest sources I could trace were articles from 2023 and 2024 about hormones at school-based health clinics in Seattle, and an aggregator post dated the day before that first remark. I should be clear that I did not see that post's text. In fact, I did not see any social media post directly. The wording I have comes from fact-checks and news reports that quote the posts, and reach figures are almost entirely missing.

A second family says the state will take your child if you don't affirm. That was said about California's Senate Bill 107, and about a Minnesota law. Both are rules about which state's courts hear a custody case. I found no case of custody taken in order to transition a child.

## 03 — Claims: how they sort, and the distortion moves

Here is how the 33 sort.

Every claim that a school or the state gave a minor medication or surgery without parents has no underlying case. A second group has a real case, or a real bill, underneath, but materially distorted. A third group is substantially accurate, and those are nearly all about social transition, written policy, or what a statute says. Oregon's age of medical consent really is 15. Non-disclosure policies really exist.

The distortions follow a few repeated moves. A device becomes medication. A referral becomes treatment. "The law permits it" becomes "it happened". One case becomes "schools everywhere". And court-authorized becomes secret.

Two more things. Some debunks overreached. Fact-checks of one California bill left out the shelter clause that critics were actually pointing at. And experts quoted in others made absolute statements that the Oregon and Maine laws contradict. On the other hand, I found no retraction by anyone who originated a surgery, hormone, or custody claim.

Last, a fresh example of mutation. On Elk Grove, the original article said suspension is reserved for severe or pervasive harassment. Three days later, a national site's version said the rule has no such qualifier.

## 04 — Messengers: who is saying what

I mapped who is saying it. The pattern is that the claim changes content at the amplification step.

Parents who went public, people filing records requests, and the lawyers bringing cases almost always describe names, pronouns, support plans, and withheld records. The operation and kidnapping versions come from rally speeches, cable hosts, and campaign ads.

And when asked directly, nobody I profiled produced a case of a school arranging medication or surgery. The Trump campaign in 2024, and the White House in 2026, answered with social-transition cases. Tiffany Justice, a co-founder of Moms for Liberty, said, quote, "Are kids getting surgery in school? No they're not." A Heritage Foundation legal fellow said he was "not aware of a specific incident yet where it has gone that far".

There's also a numbers problem. One advocacy group lists 1,219 school districts, and about 12.4 million students. That is enrollment in districts that have certain policy language. It is not a count of children affected. But it gets relayed as if it were, including in a press release for a bill in Congress.

## 05 — Messengers: what else they are saying

What else are these messengers saying? In the material I read, this claim travels with a consistent set of themes. Critical race theory and diversity programs, described as indoctrination. Women's sports and facilities. Bans on gender medicine for minors. And the line that children do not belong to the state. In ad bundles, it sits beside immigration, crime, and taxes. That's from the documents I read, not a systematic count.

Careful and sensational voices split on the same cases. After the Supreme Court's order in the California case, one conservative legal analyst called it "a procedural one, only". A school board president said "the fight is over". So the careful voices include conservatives, not only their critics.

The biggest change since 2025 is who converts claims into action. It's now the federal executive, through investigations under student-records law, and lawsuits. So far, our catalog shows one contested finding, against California, and none against a district.

In California and Orange County, what I found was policy fights, a recall, a city ordinance, and counts from records requests. Not medical cases.

One limit. For several well-known names on my list, I found no statement on this topic at all, and I won't put words in their mouths.

## 06 — Channels: the path and the speed

I traced how it moves. The documented path has four steps.

First, a local item: a teacher's own video, a clip from a school board meeting, a document from a records request, or a bill. Second, an aggregator account or advocacy outlet, with a compressed headline. Third, national partisan outlets, plus an official or a candidate. Fourth, the institution responds, and the response becomes the next story.

It's fast. In one Oklahoma case, an official amplified an aggregator's post about a day later. From a bill passing to a viral "they'll take your kids" claim took zero to three days. The first fact-check usually arrived two to eight days after the claim. Those are case observations, not a study.

The key finding is where the strong version appears. In every case I traced, the local material was about names, pronouns, clubs, records, or shelter notification rules. The surgery and child-removal versions appeared at the national stage.

There's a harder edge. NBC counted 33 instances where bomb threats or other violent intimidation followed posts by one aggregator account. NBC also said the direct inspiration for those threats is not known.

For contrast, a fully false claim, litter boxes in schools, took about a month to go national, and peaked ten months later, in campaign season.

## 07 — Channels: what is measured and what is not

On scale, here is what is actually measured.

Advertising. About $183 million, and 217,000 airings, of Republican transgender-themed ads through late October 2026, compared with $151 million at the same point in 2024. Those are tracking-firm numbers, relayed by the press.

Polling. Most polls show large majorities for schools telling parents. But in one survey, support fell to 36% when the question specified a student who feels unsafe telling them. So the wording matters a great deal.

Tip lines and lists. Where states released the data, usable complaints were a small fraction. In Indiana, a state portal published submissions, and all 11 districts that responded objected.

And corrections travel poorly. General research suggests fact-checks rarely reach the people who saw the original claim, and crowd-sourced notes tend to arrive late. Nothing measures that for these specific claims.

What is not measured at all? I found no poll asking how many people believe the surgery claim, and no view counts for any specific viral post. And several of the investigations I wanted to read, I could not open.

## 08 — Drivers: stated strategy and money

My job was the hardest to do honestly: what is driving this. So I labelled every item by how we know it. Stated by the actor. Shown by data. Reported by named sources. Asserted by an opponent. Or inference.

Start with what the actors said themselves. The head of the American Principles Project told the New York Times in 2023, quote, "We knew we needed to find an issue that the candidates were comfortable talking about", and, "we threw everything at the wall." The activist Christopher Rufo said in a 2022 speech, "You have to fight on terms that you define." And that first group's own memo to candidates, in September 2026, reported message-test shifts of up to 6.6 points, while conceding the issue alone "may not be sufficient".

Then the money. Republican television spending on these ads was reported at nearly $215 million in 2024. The largest single legal payout I found is a fee award of about $4.5 million in the California case, and that is on hold during the appeal.

Polling is an incentive in itself. Depending on the wording, support for telling parents ranges from about 40% to more than 80%.

A caution. I read those newspaper quotes in reprints, not the originals. They should be checked before anyone relies on the exact wording.

## 09 — Drivers: legal precision, political fusion, real fuel

Here is the finding I think matters most. Legal documents keep the two claims apart. Political speech fuses them.

The lawsuits allege social transition only. An Education Department letter concedes that the student-records law imposes no duty to proactively inform parents. Even the executive order on schools defines social transition as excluding medical treatment. But that same order's purpose clause speaks of schools steering students toward, quote, "surgical and chemical mutilation without parental consent". And the rally lines go further still.

There is real fuel here. Some states and districts did have written non-disclosure rules. A handful of cases have records showing conduct that continued over a parent's objection. And the medical debate shifted, with England's Cass Review, and a first jury verdict for a detransitioner.

On whether the 2026 advertising worked, I have only post-election commentary, not certified results. So I'm leaving that open.

Finally, what I could not document. Donors directing the message. Fundraising as the motive. Platform payouts to particular accounts. Foreign amplification of the school story. Those may be true or false. On the evidence I found, they are hypotheses, and I have labelled them that way.

## 10 — Opposing narrative: absolute claims against the record

I put the other side's loudest claims through the same test. 18 claims. And the result is the same in kind. The absolute versions fail. The narrow versions hold.

"No school hides anything from parents." Records contradict that. California's former state guidance told schools to respect a student's limits on disclosure, including, quote, "not sharing that information with the student's parents".

"No minors get surgery." Insurance claims data reported by Reuters show 776 mastectomies and 56 genital surgeries on minors from 2019 to 2021. Rare, but not none.

When an Oklahoma student died in 2024, early posts said the student had been beaten to death. The medical examiner ruled the death a suicide, and no charges were filed.

On "forced outing", I found predictions of homelessness and violence, but no outcome study of notification policies. One advocacy group's own research brief says the impact "cannot yet be measured".

And some outlets described the Supreme Court's March order as approving forced outing nationwide. The order covers objecting parents in California. Advocacy groups' own legal explainers say so.

A caution of my own. For several slogans, I could not find a named person saying the absolute version. I've recorded those as not located.

## 11 — Opposing narrative: messaging guides, what holds up, mirror moves

Some claims were qualified by the people making them. A civil-liberties lawyer told the Supreme Court that completed suicide, quote, "thankfully and admittedly, is rare", while arguing that studies show the treatment reduces suicidal thoughts and behavior. A major advocacy group's web page still says blockers are fully reversible. The Endocrine Society's own wording is "generally reversible". And in February 2026, the American Society of Plastic Surgeons recommended no surgery before 19.

The messaging guides are candid. One, from 2025, warns that words like "always" and "never" make messages easy to attack, and says to leave in-depth medical conversations to physicians. Another advises not repeating or rebutting the opposition's claims, and tells advocates to "name the villains".

What holds up on this side? Schools do not arrange medication or surgery. Surgery on minors is rare. Texas really did investigate parents: 11 investigations, with no removals. And threats did follow viral posts.

So the moves mirror each other. "Rare" becomes "never". A survey correlation becomes a cause. The worst reading of a law becomes its settled effect.

Two limits. Mainstream fact-checkers examined only one claim from this side; the other critiques I found came from heterodox or conservative outlets. And my searches were cut off early, so this track is thinner than the others.

## 12 — Moderator: closing

Thank you. Pulling it together.

Working inward, the wild claims shrink in a consistent way. At the outer edge: surgery at school, children seized by the state. No case found. One layer in: real bills and real cases, stretched. At the core: disputes about names, pronouns, and what parents are told. Those are real, and courts have ruled both ways.

The stretch happens mostly at one step, when a local story reaches national politics and advertising. Both sides have absolute slogans that the records contradict. And both sides have careful voices who say so.

What we still can't tell you. How many people believe the strongest claims. How far any single post travelled. And what, beyond their own stated strategy and the public numbers, motivates the people spreading them. We also never saw the original posts, only reports quoting them.
